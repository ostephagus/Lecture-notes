Suppose $\vec{x_0}$ is a fixed point of $\dvec{x} = \vec{f}(\vec{x})$. If $\vec{f}$ is smooth near $\vec{x_0}$, define the new coordinate $\vec{y} = \vec{x} - \vec{x_0}$. Taylor expansion gives:
\begin{align*}
    \dvec{y} &= \dvec{x} \text{ because $\vec{x_0}$ is constant} \\
    &= \vec{f}(\vec{x_0} + \vec{y}) \\
    &= \underbrace{\vec{f}(\vec{x_0})}_0 + A\vec{y} + O(\norm{\vec{y}}^2)
\end{align*}
where $A_{ij} = \frac{\partial f_i}{\partial x_j}$, the Jacobian of $\vec{f}$.

We can therefore approximate the flow near $\vec{x_0}$ as $\dvec{y} = A\vec{y}$.
\section{2D Flows Near Fixed Points}
In 2D, the matrix $A$ can be put into normal form, with eigenvalues given by:
\begin{equation*}
    \lambda_{1, 2} = \frac12 \left[\tr(A) \pm \sqrt{(\tr(A))^2 - 4\det(A)}\right]
\end{equation*}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Saddle points: $\det(A) < 0$. In this case $\lambda_1, \lambda_2$ are real with opposite signs. WLOG let $\lambda_1 < 0 < \lambda_2$. Also, $A$ can be diagonalised in this case. The solution for $\vec{y}$ is:
        \begin{equation*}
            \vec{y} = \vec{y_0} + \begin{pmatrix}e^{\lambda_1 t} \\ e^{\lambda_2 t}\end{pmatrix}.
        \end{equation*}
        Eliminating $t$ gives:
        \begin{equation*}
            y_2 \propto \frac{1}{y_1^{|\lambda_2 / \lambda_1|}}
        \end{equation*}
        which is a hyperbola.
    \item Node: $(\tr(A))^2 > 4 \det(A) > 0$. In this case $\lambda_1$ and $\lambda_2$ are real and have the same sign as $\tr(A)$. We can again diagonalise and eliminate $t$:
        \begin{equation*}
            y_2 \propto y_1^{\lambda_2 / \lambda_1}
        \end{equation*}
        which gives the phase space as in figure~\ref{figNodePS}. For $\tr(A) > 0$, the arrows are outward, and are inward (opposite to the figure) in the case $\tr(A) < 0$.
    \item Focus/spiral: $(\tr(A))^2 < 4\det(A)$. Here the roots are complex, $\lambda = \rho \pm i\omega$, where $\rho = \frac12 \tr(A)$. Changing to polar coordinates, we obtain:
        \begin{equation*}
            \dot{r} = \rho r\qquad\dot{\theta} = \omega
        \end{equation*}
        therefore we have spiral trajectories, which spiral inwards (stable) in the case $\rho < 0$ and spiral outwards (unstable) in the case $\rho > 0$.
    \item Repeated eigenvalue: $(\tr(A))^2 = 4\det(A)$. In this case $\lambda = \frac12 \tr(A)$ is a double root. The case splits on whether $A$ is diagonalisable:
        \begin{enumerate}
            \item Stellar node: $A$ is diagonalisable. In this case we have straight lines pointing out of the node, which are inwards or outwards depending on the sign of $\lambda$.
            \item Improper node: $A$ is given by
                \begin{equation*}
                    A = 
                    \begin{pmatrix}
                        \lambda & 1 \\
                        0 & \lambda
                    \end{pmatrix}.
                \end{equation*}
                We solve this case to give:
                \begin{equation*}
                    y_1 = \left(y_{1, 0} + y_{2, 0} t\right) e^{\lambda t},\qquad y_2 = y_{2, 0} e^{\lambda t}
                \end{equation*}
                See figure~\ref{figImproperNodePS}
        \end{enumerate}
    \item Centre: $\det(A) > 0$, $\tr(A) = 0$.
        In this case, the eigenvalues are pure-imaginary. This gives us ellipses around the fixed point (centre).
    \item Zero eigenvalue: $\det(A) = 0$, $\tr(A) \neq 0$. Label $\lambda_1 = 0, \lambda_2 = \tr(A)$. Here $y_1$ is constant, and we have a line of fixed points with trajectories perpendicular towards/away from it.
    \item Double zero eigenvalues: $\det(A) = \tr(A) = 0$. We have the case $\det(A) = 0$, which gives $y_1$ and $y_2$ constant and has a whole region around $(0, 0)$ of fixed points. There is also the case:
        \begin{equation*}
            A =
            \begin{pmatrix}
                0 & 1 \\
                0 & 0
            \end{pmatrix}
        \end{equation*}
        In this case we again have a line of fixed points, this time along $y_1 = 0$, with trajectories parallel to it.
\end{enumerate}
We have been using an eigenvector (or generalised eigenvector) basis throughout these cases. In the original coordinates, the local topology of the orbits may be distorted.

\section{Hamiltonian Systems}
\begin{definition}{Hamiltonian system}
    A dynamical system is \underline{Hamiltonian} if it has the form:
    \begin{equation*}
        \begin{pmatrix}\dot{x} \\ \dot{y}\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}\frac{\partial H}{\partial y} \\ -\frac{\partial H}{\partial x}\end{pmatrix}
    \end{equation*}
\end{definition}
In this case, linearisation gives:
\begin{equation*}
    A =
    \begin{pmatrix}
        H_{xy} & H_{yy} \\
        -H_{xx} & -H_{xy}
    \end{pmatrix}
\end{equation*}
and notably $\tr(A) = 0$. Therefore, all fixed points of a Hamiltonian system are centres ($\det(A) > 0$) or saddle points ($\det(A) < 0$).
\begin{proposition}
    On an orbit in a Hamiltonian system, $H$ is constant.
    \label{propHamiltonConserve}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
    Consider:
    \begin{align*}
        \frac{dH}{dt} &= \left(\dvec{x} \cdot \nabla\right)H \\
        &= \dot{x} H_x + \dot{y} H_y \\
        &= H_yH_x - H_x H_y = 0.
    \end{align*}
    That is, orbits follow contours of $H = \text{const}$.
\end{proof}
\section{Effect of Nonlinear Terms}
\begin{definition}{Hyperbolic fixed point}
    A fixed point $\vec{x_0}$ is \underline{hyperbolic} if none of the eigenvalues of the Jacobian at $\vec{x_0}$ have zero real part.
\end{definition}
A hyperbolic fixed point can be classified as follows.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Source: all eigenvalues have positive real part.
    \item Sink: all eigenvalues have negative real part.
    \item Saddles: some eigenvalues have different signs.
\end{itemize}
\begin{proposition}
    Let a dynamical system be given by $\dvec{x} = \vec{f}(\vec{x})$. Let $\vec{x_0}$ be a fixed point. Then if:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item $\vec{x_0}$ is a hyperbolic fixed point
        \item the nonlinear corrections in $\vec{f}(\vec{x})$ are on the order $O(\norm{\vec{x} - \vec{x_0}}^2)$
    \end{itemize}
    then the full system and the linearised system are topologically conjugate.
    \label{propHypMeansLinearOnly}
\end{proposition}
\begin{remarks}
    \item This means that for systems with quadratic nonlinear corrections, we know that the fixed points will have the same classification in the linear and nonlinear systems -- an improper node in the linear system cannot become a focus in the full system, for example.
    \item This does not apply when we have terms that are between $O(x)$ and $O(x^2)$, such as $x\log(x)$.
\end{remarks}
\subsection{Stable and Unstable Manifolds}
\begin{definition}{Stable, unstable and centre subspaces}
    The \underline{stable subspace} $E^S$, \underline{unstable subspace} $E^U$ and \underline{centre subspace} $E^S$ of the linearisation of $\vec{f}(\vec{x})$ near a fixed point $\vec{x_0}$ are the subspaces spanned by the subsets of (possibly generalised) eigenvectors of the linearisation matrix $A$ whose eigenvalues have $\Re(\lambda) < 0$, $\Re(\lambda) > 0$, $\Re(\lambda) = 0$, respectively.
\end{definition}
An equivalent characterisation of hyperbolic fixed points is that their centre subspace is the empty set.
\begin{example}
    Consider a linearisation:
    \begin{equation*}
        A =
        \begin{pmatrix}
            -1 & 0 \\
            0 & 1
        \end{pmatrix}
    \end{equation*}
    Here we are dealing with a saddle point. Then $E^S =\langle \vec{e_x}\rangle$ and $E^U = \langle \vec{e_y}\rangle$.
\end{example}
\begin{theorem}[Stable Manifold Theorem]
    Suppose that $\vec{x_0} = \vec0$ is a hyperbolic fixed point of $\dvec{x} = \vec{f}(\vec{x})$ with stable/unstable subsets $E^S$/$E^U$ near $\vec0$. Then there exist stable and unstable manifolds $W^S_{loc}$ and $W^U_{loc}$ which satisfy the following:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item $W^S_{loc}$ and $W^U_{loc}$ have the same dimension as $E^S$ and $E^U$;
        \item $W^S_{loc}$ and $W^U_{loc}$ are tangent to $E^S$ and $E^U$ at $\vec0$ such that for $\vec{x} \neq 0$ in a sufficiently small neighbourhood of $\vec0$ we have:
        \begin{align*}
            W^S_{loc} &= \subsetselect{\vec{x}}{\phi_t(\vec{x}) \to 0 \text{ as } t \to \infty} \\
            W^U_{loc} &= \subsetselect{\vec{x}}{\phi_t(\vec{x}) \to 0 \text{ as } t \to -\infty}
        \end{align*}
    \end{itemize}
    \label{thmStableManifold}
\end{theorem}
\begin{remarks}
    \item For a linear system, the stable subspace and stable manifold coincide. For nonlinear systems, the stable manifold will be initially tangent to $E^S$ but may be curved due to the effect of nonlinear terms.
    \item The local stable manifold $W^S_{loc}$ can be extended to a global stable manifold $W^S$ by following the flow backwards in time. Equivalently, it can be extended by including the orbits of all the points in $W^S_{loc}$. By construction, $W^S$ will be an invariant set. The same applies for $W^U$, by following flows forwards in time.
\end{remarks}

In the case of a saddle in $\R^2$ (where the dimension of the stable and unstable subspaces are both 1), we can provide an algorithm for obtaining approximations to $W^S_{loc}$ and $W^U_{loc}$. Let the dynamical system be defined by:
\begin{equation*}
    \begin{pmatrix}\dot{x} \\ \dot{y}\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}f(x, y) \\ g(x, y)\end{pmatrix}
\end{equation*}
and suppose $E^U = \{y = 0\}, E^S = \{x = 0\}$, after possibly transforming coordinates. We then look for $W^S_{loc}$ in the form $x = S(y)$ and $W^U_{loc}$ in the form $y = U(x)$. Note that we will require $S(0) = U(0) = S'(0) = U'(0) = 0$ to pass through the origin and to be tangent to the stable/unstable subsets.
\begin{align*}
    \dot{x} &= \frac{dS}{dy} \dot{y} = \frac{dS}{dy} g(x, y) = f(x, y) \\
    &\implies \frac{dS}{dy} g(S(y), y) = f(S(y), y)
\end{align*}
which is a nonlinear ODE for $S(y)$. The same process can be applied for the unstable manifold to get a nonlinear ODE for $U(x)$. However, we do not need to solve these ODEs because we only want to find $S$ and $U$ close to the origin. Therefore, we can substitute in convergent series expansions for $S$ and $U$ near $\vec0$ to solve these ODEs.
\begin{example}
    Consider:
    \begin{equation*}
        \begin{pmatrix}\dot{x} \\ \dot{y}\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}x(1-y) \\ -y + x^2\end{pmatrix}
    \end{equation*}
    which has linearisation matrix:
    \begin{equation*}
        A =
        \begin{pmatrix}
            (1-y) & -x \\
            2x & -1
        \end{pmatrix}
    \end{equation*}
    We consider the fixed point at the origin so:
    \begin{equation*}
        A(0, 0) = 
        \begin{pmatrix}
            1 & 0 \\
            0 & -1
        \end{pmatrix}
    \end{equation*}
    and so indeed we have a saddle point here. The eigenvectors are along the coordinate axes.

    For $W^U_{loc}$ we take $y = U(x) = a_2 x^2 + a_3 x^3 + \cdots$. Our nonlinear ODE is:
    \begin{equation*}
        -U(x) + x^2 = \frac{dU}{dx} x (1-U(x))
    \end{equation*}
    Substituting in:
    \begin{align*}
        x^2 - a_2 x^2 - a_3 x^3 - a_4 x^4 + \cdots &= \left(2 a_2 x + 3 a_3 x^2 + 4 a_4 x^3 + \cdots\right) x \left(1 - a_2 x^2 + \cdots\right) \\
        (1 - a_2) x^2 - a_3 x^3 - a_4 x^4 + \cdots &= 2 a_2 x^2  + 3a_3 x^3 + (4a_4 - 2a_2^2) x^4 + \cdots
    \end{align*}
    Then forming simultaneous equations from each term:
    \begin{align*}
        x^2 :&~1 - a_2 = 2 a_2 \implies a_2 = \frac13 \\
        x^3 :&~-a_3 = 3 a_3 \implies a_3 = 0 \\
        x^4 :&~-a_4 = 4 a_4 - 2 a_2^2 \implies a_4 = \frac2{45} \\
    \end{align*}
    and so we find an approximation $U(x) = \frac{x^2}{3} + \frac{2x^4}{45}$. Note that we could have found $a_3 = 0$ by symmetry -- the system is symmetric in $x \mapsto -x$. Therefore, when calculating $W^S$ we will find $S(y) = 0$.
\end{example}
If $E^U$, $E^S$ are one-dimensional and not aligned with the coordinate axes, we can instead write $S$ and $U$ tangent to the eigenvectors. For example, if $\begin{pmatrix}1 \\ k\end{pmatrix}$ is a stable eigenvector, we can write:
\begin{equation*}
    S(x) = kx + a_2 x^2 + a_3 x^3 + \cdots
\end{equation*}
In higher dimensions, we use multivariate $S$ and $U$. Note that we still require $S$ and $U$ non-empty.
\begin{example}
    Suppose that we have a 3D dynamical system, with $\vec{e_x}$ and $\vec{e_y}$ stable, $\vec{e_z}$ unstable. We can write $z = S(x, y)$ and solve as before.
\end{example}
\subsection{Nonlinear Terms in the Non-Hyperbolic Case}
Now we suppose that at least one eigenvalue has zero real part.

\begin{example}[Centre at the origin]
    Consider the simple system:
    \begin{equation*}
        \dot{x} = -y,\qquad\dot{y} = x
    \end{equation*}
    Which has Hamiltonian $H = -\frac12(x^2 + y^2)$. Therefore, since orbits are the contours of the Hamiltonian, the orbits are simply circles around the origin. Now consider the nonlinear system:
    \begin{align*}
        \dot{x} &= -y -x^3 \\
        \dot{y} &= x - y^3
    \end{align*}
    The linearisation lends itself to polar coordinates, so we convert the nonlinear system:
    \begin{align*}
        \dot{r} &= \frac{x^4 + y^4}{r} & \dot{\theta} &= 1 - \frac{xy^3 - yx^3}{r^2}
    \end{align*}
    Now note that $\dot{r} < 0$, and as $r \to 0$, $\dot{\theta} \to 1$.

    This is a stable focus, but different to those seen when linearising. The rate of decay in $r$ is slower than the exponential decay we saw with linear systems.

    Consider another possibility (both cubic terms positive):
    \begin{align*}
        \dot{x} &= -y + x^3 \\
        \dot{y} &= x + y^3
    \end{align*}
    Here $\dot{r} = \frac{x^4 + y^4}{r} > 0$, so we have an unstable focus.

    We also consider the case of a mixed nonlinear term:
    \begin{align*}
        \dot{x} &= -y - 2x^2 y \\
        \dot{y} &= x + 2y^2 x
    \end{align*}
    and this has Hamiltonian $H = -\frac12 \left(x^2 + y^2\right) - x^2 y^2$. Close to the origin we still have circles (approximately), but moving further out we get diamond shapes with rounded corners. Therefore, in this case the origin is still a centre but the trajectories around it are no longer circular.
\end{example}
\begin{example}[Line of fixed points at the origin]
    Consider the case $\lambda_1 = 0, \lambda_2 \neq 0$. WLOG set $\dot{x} = 0$ in the linearisation.

    We can consider the system:
    \begin{equation*}
        \dot{x} = x^2,\qquad \dot{y} = -y
    \end{equation*}
    which has an exact solution, with $y \propto e^{1 / x}$. We see that the phase portrait is as in figure~\ref{figNLSaddleNode}.

    We can increase the power of $x$ to get:
    \begin{equation*}
        \dot{x} = x^3,\qquad \dot{y} = -y
    \end{equation*}   
    which has solution $y\propto \exp\left(\frac{1}{2x^2}\right)$ and gives a saddle point.
\end{example}

\subsection{Sketching Phase Portraits}
We can now provide an algorithm for sketching phase portraits.
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Check for symmetries ($y \mapsto -y$, or $x\mapsto y$) and find the fixed points.
    \item Calculate the Jacobian and classify the fixed points.
    \item For saddles and nodes, find the eigenvectors.
    \item Find nullclines (curves where $\dot{x} = 0$ or $\dot{y} = 0$) and get the flow directions (sign of the nonzero time derivative) on them.
    \item Use the knowledge of stable and unstable manifolds to plot nonlinear flows.
    \item Combine the trajectories around fixed points into a global picture.
    \item Remember: trajectories cannot cross!
    \item Search for periodic orbits.
\end{enumerate}
