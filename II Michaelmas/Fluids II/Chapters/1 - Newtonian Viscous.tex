\section{Dynamic Viscosity}
We recall Couette flow, as in figure~\ref{figCouetteCell}.
\begin{figure}
    \centering

        \pgfmathsetmacro{\h}{2}
        \pgfmathsetmacro{\flowlinespacing}{0.2}

        \draw (0, 0) -- (5, 0);
        \draw[->] (0, \h) -- (5, \h) node[right] {$U$};
        \draw[|-|] (-0.5, 0) -- (-0.5, \h) node[anchor=east, pos=0.5] {$h$};

        \pgfmathsetmacro{\numflowlines}{\h / \flowlinespacing - 1}

        \foreach \n [evaluate=\n as \y using \n*\flowlinespacing] in {1, 2, ..., \numflowlines} {
            \draw[->, blue] (2, \y) -- +(\y, 0);
        }
    \end{tikzpicture}
    \caption{Diagram of the Couette Cell}
    \label{figCouetteCell}
\end{figure}
In this example we consider 3D flow, and so figure~\ref{figCouetteCell} is a cross-section of flow between two plates. For simple, Newtonian fluids, we find that the force required to move top plate is proportional to speed and area, but inversely proportional to the height:
\begin{equation*}
    F \propto \frac{UA}{h}.
\end{equation*}
Hence, the shear stress $\tau$ (the force per unit area) is proportional to area and inversely proportional to the height. The constant of proportionality is the dynamic viscosity $\mu$:
\begin{equation*}
    \tau = \tau \frac{U}{h}
\end{equation*}
and the units are $Pa~s$ or $kg~m^{-1}s^{-1}$. For context, air has $\mu = 2\times10^{-5}$, water has $\mu = 10^{-3}$, olive oil has $\mu = 0.1$ and golden syrup has $\mu = 200$.

Figure~\ref{figCouetteCell} shows the velocity profile of the fluid. Shear stress is due to the relative motion between each layer of fluid and its neighbour layers above and below. That is, the shear stress depends on the gradient of $u$.
\section{Rate of Strain Tensor}
We consider the velocity variations near a fixed point which, by translating our coordinates, is at the origin. A Taylor expansion of the velocity field gives:
\begin{align*}
    u_i(\vec{x}) &= u_i(\vec0) + x_j \frac{\partial u_i}{\partial x_j} + \frac12 u_j x_k \frac{\partial^{2}u_i}{\partial x_j\partial x_k} + \cdots \\
    &\approx u_i(\vec0) + x_j \frac{\partial u_i}{\partial x_j} \text{ near the fixed point} \\
    &= u_i(\vec0) + \frac12 \left[\left(\frac{\partial u_i}{\partial x_j} + \frac{\partial u_j}{\partial x_i}\right) + \frac12 \left(\frac{\partial u_i}{\partial x_j} - \frac{\partial u_j}{\partial x_i}\right)\right] x_j \\
    &= u_i(\vec0) + e_{ij}x_j + \frac12 \epsilon_{jik} \omega_k x_j
\end{align*}
where $e$ is the \underline{rate of strain tensor} (rank-2) and $\vec{\omega} = \nabla \times \vec{u}$ is the vorticity. Returning to the equation, and writing in vector form:
\begin{equation*}
    \vec{u}(\vec{x}) \approx \underbrace{\vec{u}(0)}_{\substack{\text{constant}\\\text{translation}}} + \underbrace{e \vec{x}}_{\substack{\text{strain /}\\\text{deformation}}} + \underbrace{\frac12 \vec{\omega} \times \vec{x}}_{\substack{\text{solid body}\\\text{rotation}}}.
\end{equation*}
Note that $e$ is real and symmetric, so must have real, orthogonal eigenvectors.
\begin{definition}{Principle axes of strain}
    The eigenvectors of $e$ are the \underline{principle axes of strain}.
\end{definition}
Note also that $\operatorname{tr}(e) = \nabla \cdot \vec{u}$, so the eigenvalues must sum to zero.
\begin{definition}{Principle rates of strain}
    The eigenvalues of $e$ are the \underline{principle rates of strain}.
\end{definition}
\begin{example}[Plane straining flow]
    Consider the flow:
    \begin{equation*}
        \vec{u} = \left(\alpha x, -\alpha y, 0\right).
    \end{equation*}
    Then the rate of strain tensor is:
    \begin{equation*}
        e =
        \begin{pmatrix}
            \alpha & 0 & 0 \\
            0 & -\alpha & 0 \\
            0 & 0 & 0
        \end{pmatrix}
    \end{equation*}
    thereby giving flow lines $y = \frac{k}{x}$.
\end{example}
\begin{example}[Simple shear flow]
    Consider flow with:
    \begin{equation*}
        \vec{u} = (\gamma y, 0, 0)
    \end{equation*}
    then this gives:
    \begin{equation*}
        e =
        \begin{pmatrix}
            0 & \frac\gamma2 & 0 \\
            \frac\gamma2 & 0 & 0 \\
            0 & 0 & 0
        \end{pmatrix}
    \end{equation*}
    which has eigenvectors along the diagonals.
\end{example}
\section{Stress Tensor}
We consider two types of forces:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item body forces $\vec{f}$ that act per unti volume, such as gravity;
    \item surface forces $\vec\tau$ that act per unit area, such as pressure.
\end{enumerate}
Let $\vec\tau(\vec{n}; \vec{x}, t)~\delta S$ denote the force exerted by the $+$ side on the $-$ side of a small area element $\vec{n}~dS$. Now, let $\vec{n}~\delta S$ be the sloping face of a small tetrahedron of size $l$, whose other 3 faces are aligned with the coordinate planes.

Consider the force balance on this tetrahedron:
\begin{itemize}
    \item the body force and inertia are $O(l^3)$;
    \item surface forces are $O(l^2)$;
    \item the ratio between these forces is therefore $O(l)$, and so surface forces are more important as $l \to 0$.
\end{itemize}
Therefore the surface forces must balance themselves when $l$ is small, therefore:
\begin{equation*}
    \vec\tau(\vec{n})~\delta S + \vec{\tau}(-\vec{e_1})~\delta S_1 + \vec\tau(-\vec{e_2})~\delta S_2 + \vec{\tau}(-\vec{e_3})~\delta S_3 = 0.
\end{equation*}
By the geometry of the tetrahedron, $\delta S_i = n_i \delta S$, and by Newton's 3rd Law $\vec{\tau}(-\vec{e_i}) = -\vec\tau(\vec{e_i})$. Therefore,
\begin{equation*}
    \vec\tau(\vec{n}) = \vec\tau(\vec{e_1}) n_1 + \vec\tau(\vec{e_2}) n_2 + \vec\tau(\vec{e_3}) n_3
\end{equation*}
which is linear in $\vec{n}$, so we can write:
\begin{equation*}
    \tau_i(\vec{n}) = \sigma_{ij} n_j
\end{equation*}
where $\sigma_{ij} = \tau_i(\vec{e_j})$ are the components of the \underline{stress tensor} $\sigma(\vec{x}, t)$.

The key result here is that the stress $\vec\tau$ exerted on a small area element is given by:
\begin{equation*}
    \vec\tau(\vec{n}) = \sigma\vec{n}
\end{equation*}

The symmetry of $\sigma_{ij}$ can be seen by considering the angular momentum balance on a small arbitrary volume $V$ (characteristic length $l$) about a fixed point in $V$. WLOG consider this to be the origin. The moment of the surface forces is then:
\begin{equation*}
    \int_{\partial V} \epsilon_{ijk} x_j \sigma_{kl} n_l~dS
\end{equation*}
which is $O(l^3)$. The moment of volume forces and inertia is:
\begin{equation*}
    \int_V \vec{x} \times \vec{f}~dV
\end{equation*}
which is $O(l^4)$. We can therefore invoke the same argument:
\begin{align*}
    0 &= \int_{\partial V} \epsilon_{ijk} x_j \sigma_{kl} n_l~dS \\
    \text{Divergence thm: } &= \int_V \epsilon_{ijk} \frac{\partial}{\partial x_l} \left(x_j \sigma_{kl}\right) dV \\
    &= \int_V \epsilon_{ijk} \left(\sigma_{kj} + x_j \frac{\partial \sigma_{kl}}{\partial x_l}\right) dV
\end{align*}
then the second term in this integral is $O(l^4)$ where the first is $O(l^3)$, so by the previous logic we remove it:
\begin{equation*}
    0 = \int_V \epsilon_{ijk} \sigma_{kj}~dV
\end{equation*}
However, since $V$ was arbitrary we in fact must have $\epsilon_{ijk} \sigma_{kj} = 0$ which requires $\sigma$ to be symmetric.
\section{Constitutive Equation for a Newtonian Fluid}
A constitutive equation is one that describes how something behaves based on what it is made of. This equation describes the relation between stress and strain for a linear, viscous fluid. We first split the stress tensor $\sigma_{ij}$ into two terms.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Isotropic pressure term: $-p\delta_{ij}$ where $p = -\frac13 \sigma_{kk}$.
    \item Deviatoric stress term: $\sigma_{ij}^{\text{dev}}$ given by $\sigma_{ij}^\text{dev} = \sigma_{ij} + p \delta_{ij}$. This models the frictional forces in the fluid, due to adjacent fluid particles sliding past at differing velocities.
\end{itemize}
Fluid at rest has $\sigma_{ij}^\text{dev} = 0$, with just a static pressure $\nabla p = \vec{f}$.

Newtonian fluids are defined by:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item $\sigma^\text{dev}$ is linear in the local, instantaneous value of $\nabla \vec{u}$.
        The most general form is:
        \begin{equation*}
            \sigma_{ij}^\text{dev} = A_{ijkl} \frac{\partial u_k}{\partial x_l}
        \end{equation*}
        for some tensor $A_{ijkl}$.

        As an aside, local and instantaneous requires molecular length scale of $\sim 10^9~m$ and timescale of interactions $\sim 10^{-12}~s$. A suspension of wood fibres (wood pulp) has non-local interactions because the fibres act as force carries at larger length scales.
    \item The fluid is locally isotropic. That is, $A_{ijkl}$ is isotropic, and so must be given by:
        $A_{ijkl} = \mu' \delta_{ij} \delta_{kl} + \mu'' \delta_{ik} \delta{jl} + \mu''' \delta_{il}\delta_{jk}$.
\end{enumerate}
We therefore have:
\begin{equation*}
    \sigma_{ij}^\text{dev} = \mu' \delta_{ij} \underbrace{\frac{\partial u_k}{\partial x_k}}_{\nabla \cdot \vec{u} = 0} + \mu'' \frac{\partial u_j}{\partial x_i} + \mu''' \frac{\partial u_i}{\partial x_j}
\end{equation*}
Also, by symmetry, we must have that $\mu'' = \mu'''$. Therefore, we call this $\mu$ and, after adding back in the isotropic pressure,
\begin{align}
    \sigma_{ij} &= -p \delta_{ij} + \mu \left(\frac{\partial u_j}{\partial x_i} + \frac{\partial u_i}{\partial x_j}\right) \nonumber \\
    &= -p \delta_{ij} + 2\mu e_{ij} \label{eqnConstitutive}
\end{align}
\Eqnref{eqnConstitutive} is known as the constitutive relationship or stress-strain relationship for a Newtonian viscous fluid.

\begin{remarks}
    \item There is no dependence here on vorticity. The stress tensor only depends on the strain rate $e = \frac12 \left(\nabla \vec{u} + \nabla \vec{u}^T\right)$, since local solid-body rotation $\frac12 \vec{\omega} \times \vec{x}$ does not involve sliding or friction.
    \item Many common fluids are Newtonian and obey \eqnref{eqnConstitutive}. Examples: air, water, syrup, oil.
    \item $\mu$ here is the viscosity! We can see this by considering $\vec{u} = (u(y), 0, 0)^T$ which gives:
        \begin{equation*}
            \sigma_{12} = \mu \frac{\partial u}{\partial y}
        \end{equation*}
        from \eqnref{eqnConstitutive}, as in the examples of IB Fluid Dynamics.
\end{remarks}
\section{The Momentum Equation}
\subsection{The Cauchy Momentum Equation}
Change in momentum in a small, fixed volume $V$ can occur due to:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item momentum being carried across the boundary $\partial V$;
    \item body forces acting on $V$;
    \item surface forces acting on $\partial V$.
\end{enumerate}
This gives us the equation:
\begin{align*}
    \frac{d}{dt} \int_V \rho u_i~dV &= - \int_{\partial V} \rho u_i (\vec{u} \cdot \vec{n})~dS + \int_V f_i~dV + \int_{\partial V} \sigma_{ij} n_j~dS \\
    \intertext{We use that $V$ is fixed, and apply the divergence theorem where we have $\delta S$:}
    \int_V \frac{d}{dt} \rho u_i~dV &= - \int_V \frac{\partial}{\partial x_j} \left(\rho u_i u_j\right)dV + \int_V f_i~dV + \int_V \frac{\partial}{\partial x_j}\sigma_{ij} dV 
\end{align*}
Rearranging:
\begin{equation*}
    \int_V u_i \underbrace{\left(\frac{\partial \rho}{\partial t} + \frac{\partial}{\partial x_j}(\rho u_j)\right)}_{=0 \text{ by mass conservation}} dV + \int_V \rho \underbrace{\left(\frac{\partial u_i}{\partial t} + u_j \frac{\partial u_i}{\partial x_j}\right)}_{\frac{D \vec{u}}{D t}}= \int_V \left(f_i + \frac{\partial \sigma_{ij}}{\partial x_j}\right)
\end{equation*}
Then since $V$ was arbitrary we can remove the integrals:
\begin{equation}
    \rho \frac{D \vec{u}}{D t} = \vec{f} + \nabla \cdot \sigma
    \label{eqnCauchyMom}
\end{equation}
where we define:
\begin{equation*}
    \left[\nabla \cdot \sigma\right]_i \equiv \frac{\partial \sigma_{ij}}{\partial x_j}.
\end{equation*}
\Eqnref{eqnCauchyMom} is the Cauchy momentum equation. Note that we did not use incompressibility here, only conservation of mass, so this applies to any fluid continuum (including compressible fluids such as compressible gases).
\subsection{The Navier-Stokes Equation}
For a compressible, Newtonian fluid, $\sigma_{ij} = -p\delta_{ij} + 2 \mu e_{ij}$. We assume that $\mu$ is uniform, and so:
\begin{align*}
    \frac{\partial \sigma_{ij}}{\partial x_j} &= -\frac{\partial p}{\partial x_j} \delta_{ij} + \mu \frac{\partial^{2}u_i}{\partial x_j\partial x_j} + \mu \underbrace{\frac{\partial^{2}u_j}{\partial x_j\partial x_i}}_{0 \text{ since } \nabla \cdot \vec{u} = 0} \\
    &= -\frac{\partial p}{\partial x_i} + \mu \frac{\partial^{2}u_i}{\partial x_j\partial x_j}
\end{align*}
Therefore we update \eqnref{eqnCauchyMom}:
\begin{equation}
    \rho \frac{D \vec{u}}{D t} = \vec{f} - \nabla p + \mu \nabla^2 \vec{u}
    \label{eqnNavierStokes}
\end{equation}
which is the Navier-Stokes equation. Note that this is the same as the Euler equation for an inviscid fluid (see the course IB Fluid Dynamics) with an extra viscosity term, $\mu \nabla^2 \vec{u}$.
\subsection{Boundary Conditions}
We consider a material boundary with normal $\vec{n}$. This may be a boundary between the fluid and a solid, or between the fluid and a different fluid such as air.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Mass conservation across the boundary implies that $\vec{u} \cdot \vec{n}$ is continuous (no penetration condition).
    \item $\nabla \vec{u}$ must be finite, otherwise the stress $\sigma$ would be infinite. This requires the tangential velocity, $\vec{u} - (\vec{u} \cdot \vec{n})\vec{n}$, to be continuous (no slip condition).
\end{enumerate}
Therefore, we require that $\vec{u}$ is continuous across the boundary (kinematic boundary condition).
\begin{enumerate}
    \setcounter{enumi}{2}
    \item Newton's 3rd Law requires that $\sigma\vec{n}$ is continuous across the boundary (dynamic boundary condition).
\end{enumerate}
\section{Energy Equation and Viscous Dissipation}
Recalling that in simple dynamics we have $P = Fv$ - power (rate of change of energy) is the force multiplied by the velocity, we can obtain the rate of change of energy by dotting the Cauchy momentum equation with $\vec{u}$.
\begin{equation*}
    u_i \rho \left(\frac{\partial u_i}{\partial t} + u_j \frac{\partial u_i}{\partial x_j}\right) = u_i f_i + u_i \frac{\partial \sigma_{ij}}{\partial x_j}
\end{equation*}
We can look first at the LHS:
\begin{align*}
    u_i \rho \left(\frac{\partial u_i}{\partial t} + u_j \frac{\partial u_i}{\partial x_j}\right) &= \frac{\partial}{\partial t} \left(\frac12 \rho |\vec{u}|^2\right) - \frac12 |\vec{u}|^2 \frac{\partial \rho}{\partial t} + \frac{\partial}{\partial x_j} \left(\frac12 \rho |\vec{u}|^2 u_j\right) - \frac12 |\vec{u}|^2 \frac{\partial}{\partial x_j} \left(\rho u_j\right) \\
    &= \frac{\partial}{\partial t} \left(\frac12 \rho |\vec{u}|^2\right) + \frac{\partial}{\partial x_j} \left(\frac12 \rho |\vec{u}|^2 u_j\right) \text{ by mass conservation}.
\end{align*}
Now the RHS:
\begin{align*}
    u_i f_i + u_i \frac{\partial \sigma_{ij}}{\partial x_j} &= u_i f_i + \frac{\partial}{\partial x_j}\left(u_i \sigma_{ij}\right) - \sigma_{ij} \frac{\partial u_i}{\partial x_j} \\
    &= u_i f_i + \frac{\partial}{\partial x_j}\left(u_i \sigma_{ij}\right) - \frac12\left(\sigma_{ij} \frac{\partial u_i}{\partial x_j} + \sigma_{ji} \frac{\partial u_j}{\partial x_i}\right) \\
    &= u_i f_i + \frac{\partial}{\partial x_j}\left(u_i \sigma_{ij}\right) - \frac12\left(\sigma_{ij} \frac{\partial u_i}{\partial x_j} + \sigma_{ij} \frac{\partial u_j}{\partial x_i}\right) \\
    &= u_i f_i + \frac{\partial}{\partial x_j}\left(u_i \sigma_{ij}\right) - \frac12\sigma_{ij}\left(\frac{\partial u_i}{\partial x_j} + \frac{\partial u_j}{\partial x_i}\right) \\
    &= u_i f_i + \frac{\partial}{\partial x_j}\left(u_i \sigma_{ij}\right) - \frac12\sigma_{ij} e_{ij}
\end{align*}
Integrating over a fixed volume $V$:
\begin{align*}
    \frac{d}{dt} \int_V \frac12 \rho |\vec{u}|^2~dV~+ \int_{\partial V} \frac12 \rho |\vec{u}|^2 \vec{u} &\cdot \vec{n}~dS = \int_V \vec{u} \cdot \vec{F}~dV \\
    &+ \int_{\partial V} \vec{u} \cdot (\sigma \vec{n}) dS~- \int_V \sigma_{ij} e_{ij}~dV
\end{align*}
These terms are:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item rate of change of kinetic energy inside $V$;
    \item kinetic energy being carried across the boundary $\partial V$;
    \item rate of doing work by body forces in $V$;
    \item rate of doing work by surface forces $\vec{\tau} = \sigma\vec{n}$ on $\partial V$;
    \item rate of doing work against internal stresses $\sigma$ due to deformation.
\end{enumerate}
\begin{remarks}
    \item Term 5 is non-obvious and can only really be seen from the equations we have manipulated.
    \item This was derived from \eqnref{eqnCauchyMom} (the Cauchy momentum equation), so applies for any fluid.
\end{remarks}
For an incompressible Newtonian fluid, $\sigma_{ij} - p \delta_{ij} + 2 \mu e_{ij}$, so the final term looks like:
\begin{align*}
    \sigma_{ij} e_{ij} &= \underbrace{-p e_{ii}}_{0~\because~\nabla \cdot \vec{u} = 0} + 2 \mu e_{ij} e_{ij} \\
    &= 2\mu e_{ij}e_{ij}
\end{align*}
so term 5 gives the rate of loss of mechanical energy (dissipation) due to viscosity and deformation. This energy is released as heat. We label this term:
\begin{equation*}
    \Phi = 2\mu e_{ij}e_{ij}
\end{equation*}
\section{Nondimensionalisation}
Suppose a flow has a characteristic length scale $L$, velocity scale $U$, and varies on a timescale $T$. For example, flow of speed $U$ flowing past a rigid body of characteristic length $L$ that vibrates with period $T$. We can assume that the flow is given by:
\begin{equation*}
    \vec{u} = \vec{u}(\vec{x}, t, \rho, \mu, U, L, T, \text{shape}, \cdots)
\end{equation*}
which is a lot of parameters. Define a dimensionless velocity $\vec{u}^* = \vec{u} / U$, and do the same with position $\vec{x}^*$, time $t^*$. We can now also find the pressure:
\begin{align*}
    p^* = \frac{p}{\mu U/L} \text{ (viscous scaling) or } \frac{p}{\rho U^2} \text{ (inertial scaling)}
\end{align*}
We will use the viscous scaling. \Eqnref{eqnNavierStokes} (Navier-Stokes equation) now becomes:
\begin{align}
    \rho \left(\frac{U}{T} \frac{\partial u^*}{\partial t^*} + \frac{U^2}{L} \vec{u}^* \cdot \nabla^* \vec{u}^*\right) &= -\frac{\mu U}{L^2} \nabla^2 p^{*2} + \frac{\mu U}{L^2} \nabla^{* 2} \vec{u}^* \nonumber \\
    \Re \left(\St\frac{\partial u^*}{\partial t^*} +  \vec{u}^* \cdot \nabla^* \vec{u}^*\right) &= -\nabla^2 p^{*2} + \nabla^{* 2} \vec{u}^* \label{eqnNavierStokesDimless} \\
\end{align}
where $\Re = \frac{\rho U L}{\mu} = \frac{UL}{\nu}$ is the Reynolds number and $\St = \frac{L}{UT}$ is the Stroughal number. Both are dimensionless. We can see the improvement:
\begin{equation*}
    \vec{u}^*(\vec{x}^*, t, \Re, \St, \text{shape})
\end{equation*}
which is fewer parameters. In fact, if the flow is steady we can use the advection time $T = L / U$. This means $\St = 1$ and $\Re$ is the defining characteristic. If the boundary conditions are oscillatory, we can set $T = \frac1\omega$ and note that $\Re_T = \Re\St$ (the unsteady Reynolds number) which is given by:
\begin{equation*}
    \Re_T = \frac{L^2}{\nu T} = \frac{\omega L^2}{\nu}
\end{equation*}
is comparing the length scale of the body with the length scale that the oscillation affects.

\begin{definition}{Dynamical Similarity}
    If two flows with different $U, L, T$ have the same geometry, Reynolds number, (Strall number if oscillatory) and dimensionless boundary conditions, they are said to be \underline{dynamically similar} because they give the same dimensionless $\vec{u}^*$.
\end{definition}

We can look at certain important limits:
\begin{itemize}
    \item In the case of slow oscillations (relative to the advection time), we have $\St \ll 1$ and we can neglect the term $\frac{\partial \vec{u}^*}{\partial t^*}$. This leads to quasi-steady flow.
    \item In the case of rapid oscillations (relative to the advection time), we have $\St \gg 1$ and we can neglect the term $(\vec{u}^* \cdot \nabla)\vec{u}^*$. This leads to a linear equation which is easier to solve.
    \item In the case of no oscillations, $\St = 0$. This gives two limits in terms of $\Re$:
        \begin{enumerate}
            \item $\Re \ll 1$ -- the flow is viscously dominated and so we can neglect the LHS of \eqnref{eqnNavierStokesDimless}. This gives the Stokes equation $-\nabla p = \nabla^2\vec{u}$, which is discussed later.
            \item $\Re \gg 1$ -- the fluid is inertially dominated on a length scale $L$. This means we can often neglect $\nabla^2 \vec{u}$, which is the Euler equation of IB Fluid Dynamics. However, near the boundary we see viscous effects, leading to the discussion of boundary layers later in this course.
        \end{enumerate}
\end{itemize}
