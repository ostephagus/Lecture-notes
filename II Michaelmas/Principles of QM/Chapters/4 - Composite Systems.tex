\section{Tensor Products}
This is a general way of constructing quantum systems from simpler subsystems.

Suppose:
\begin{equation*}
    \psi \in V_1,\qquad\ket\phi \in V_2
\end{equation*}
which are states in spaces for two systems. The tensor product space is written as:
\begin{equation*}
    V = V_1 \otimes V_2
\end{equation*}
which consists of all linear combinations of all tensor product states:
\begin{equation*}
    \state \otimes \ket\phi
\end{equation*}
subject to:
\begin{align*}
    \left(\state + \ket{\psi'}\right) \otimes \ket\phi &= \state \otimes \ket\phi + \ket{\psi'} \otimes \ket{\phi} \\
    \state \otimes \left(\ket{\phi} + \ket{\phi'}\right) &= \state \otimes \ket\phi + \state \otimes \ket{\phi'} \\
    \left(\alpha \state\right) \otimes \ket\phi &= \alpha \left(\state \otimes \ket\phi\right) \\
    \state \otimes \left(\alpha \ket\phi\right)&= \alpha \left(\state \otimes \ket\phi\right)
\end{align*}
and similarly for duals. This means that this is not quite a Cartesian product of states because we can move scalars $\alpha$ around. The inner product on $V$ satisfies:
\begin{equation*}
    \left(\bra{\psi'} \otimes \bra{\phi'}\right) \left(\state \otimes \ket\phi\right) = \inn{\psi'}{\psi} \inn{\phi'}{\phi}
\end{equation*}
and we extend to everything else by linearity.

Given operators $A$ and $B$ in $V_1$ and $V_2$, we define an operator $A \otimes B$ on $V$ by:
\begin{equation*}
    \left(A\otimes B\right)\left(\phi \otimes \psi\right) = \left(A\state\right)\otimes (B\ket{\phi})
\end{equation*}
and again we extend to everything else by linearity.

We can then think of $A$ in $V_1$ as an operator $A \otimes 1$ in $V$. Similarly for $B$. Operators of the form $A \otimes 1$, $1 \otimes B$ commute for any $A$, $B$.
\begin{example}
    Consider a particle in 2D with independent position operators $\hat{x}_1, \hat{x}_2$. We introduce a basis of joint eigenstates which can be constructed as:
    \begin{equation*}
        \ket{x_1, x_2} = \ket{x_1} \otimes \ket{x_2}
    \end{equation*}
    where $\ket{x_1} \in V_1$ and $\ket{x_2} \in V_2$ and $V_1, V_2$ are the spaces of particles in one dimension. We have therefore found commuting operators $\hat{x}_1 \otimes 1$ and $1 \otimes \hat{x}_2$. The wavefunction for $\psi \otimes \phi$ is now given by:
    \begin{equation*}
        \bra{x_1, x_2}\left(\state \otimes \ket{\phi}\right) = \inn{x_1}{\psi} \inn{x_2}{\psi} = \psi(x_1) \phi(x_2)
    \end{equation*}
\end{example}
We have tensor product states corresponding to separable wavefunctions and the tensor product space corresponding to all linear combinations of such wavefunctions. This motivates the idea of separable solutions.

\begin{remark}
    If we have bases $\{\ket{n}\}$ for $V_1$ and $\{\ket{m}\}$ for $V_2$, then $\{\ket{n} \otimes \ket{m}\}$ is a basis for $V$. For finite bases,
    \begin{equation*}
        \dim(V) = \dim(V_1) \times \dim(V_2).
    \end{equation*}
\end{remark}
\begin{example}[Two dimensional (isotropic) oscillator]
    Consider:
    \begin{equation*}
        H = \frac{\hat{p}_1^2 + \hat{p}_2^2}{2m} + \frac1m m \omega^2\left(\hat{x}_1^2 + \hat{x}_2^2\right) = H_1 + H_2
    \end{equation*}
    where $H_1$ and $H_2$ are Hamiltonians for subsystems that correspond to motion in only one dimension. Recall that $H_i = \hbar \omega\left(N_i + \frac12\right)$, where $N_i = a_i^\dagger a_i$ and $[a_i a_j^\dagger] = \delta_{ij}$.

    We therefore have a complete commuting set of operators, $N_1$ and $N_2$. We can construct simulatanous eigenstates as follows:
    \begin{equation*}
        \ket{n_1, n_2} = \ket{n_1} \otimes \ket{n_2}
    \end{equation*}
    and then we have energies:
    \begin{align*}
        H\ket{n_1, n_2} &= \left(H_1 \ket{n_1}\right) \otimes \ket{n_2} + \ket{n_1} \otimes \left(H_2 \ket{n_2}\right) \\
        &= E_{n_1, n_2} \ket{n_1, n_2}
    \end{align*}
    where $E_{n_1, n_2} = \hbar \omega \left(n_1 + n_2 + 1\right)$. The two-dimensional wavefunctions are given by:
    \begin{equation*}
        \psi_{n_1, n_2}(x_1, x_2) = \psi_{n_1}(x_1) \psi_{n_2}(x_2)
    \end{equation*}
    as expected.
\end{example}
\begin{remark}
    We have a slight abuse of notation in that we often write $H_1$ instead of $H_1 \otimes 1$. Further, sometimes $\ket{n_1} \otimes \ket{n_2}$ is written as $\ket{n_1} \ket{n_2}$.
\end{remark}
\section{Spin}
Experiment shows that particles generally carry an internal degree of freedom called \underline{spin} or intrinsic angular momentum. Even if a particle appears to be ``elementary'' or ``point-like'', its space of states is of the form:
\begin{equation*}
    V = V_{\text{space}} \otimes V_{\text{spin}}
\end{equation*}
with basis states $\ket{\vec{x}, r} = \ket{\vec{x}} \otimes \ket{r}$ where $r$ takes a finite set of values. This means position operators are not, in general, a complete commuting set -- we require an observable that can distinguish spin states for completeness. In this chapter we will focus on the states, not the operators.

Each kind of particle has a definite total spin $s$, which is a half-integer:
\begin{equation*}
    s \in \left\{0, \frac12, 1, \frac32, \cdots\right\}
\end{equation*}
and is part of the nature of the particle, like mass or charge. For spin $s$, a particle of non-zero mass has $2s+1$ basis states in $V_{\text{spin}}$, labelled $r = s, s-1, \cdots, -s$ by convention.
\begin{example}
    A spin-$0$ object only has one spin state $\ket0$, so it is in fact labelled entirely by its position.

    More common spin-$\frac12$ objects have two spin state $\ket{\frac12}$ and $\ket{-\frac12}$ which are more commonly referred to as ``up'' and ``down'', $\upst, \dnst$.

    Spin-$1$ objects have state $\ket1, \ket0, \ket{-1}$.
\end{example}
The existence of spin states is revealed by experiments like the Stern-Gerlach experiment, which uses a beam of particles passing through an inhomogeneous magnetic field, and the beam is split into two depending on the different spin states (we have half-spin particles in this case).

A general state $\state \in V$ now has a $(2s+1)$-component wavefunction given by:
\begin{equation*}
    \psi(\vec{x}, r) = \inn{\vec{x}, r}{\psi}
\end{equation*}
where $\abs{\psi(\vec{x}, r)}^2$ is now the probability density for the position of the particle given that its spin is $r$. Mixed/hybrid notation is also common such as:
\begin{equation*}
    \sum_r \psi(\vec{x}, r)\ket{r}
\end{equation*}
\section{Multiparticle States: Bosons and Fermions}
Consider particles labelled by $a = 1, 2, \cdots, N$ and let $V_a$ be the space of states for each one with basis $\{\ket{\vec{x_a}, r_a}\}$. General multiparticle states belong to:
\begin{equation*}
    V = V_1 \otimes V_2 \otimes \cdots \otimes V_N
\end{equation*}
with basis states $\ket{\vec{x_1}, r_1, \vec{x_2}, r_2, \cdots, \vec{x_n}, r_n} = \ket{\vec{x_1}, r_1} \otimes \cdots \otimes \ket{\vec{x_n}, r_n}$.
\subsection{Identical Particles: Bose-Einstein and Fermi-Dirac\texorpdfstring{\\}{ }Statistics}
If the $N$ particles are identical then $V_a \cong V$.
\begin{example}[2 particles]
    \label{ex2Particle}
    Take $N = 2$ and define and operator $U$ which exchanges particles by action on basis states:
    \begin{equation*}
        U\ket{\vec{x_1}, r_1, \vec{x_2}, r_2} = \ket{\vec{x_2}, r_2, \vec{x_1}, r_1}
    \end{equation*}
    We have said that these particles are identical and so the states must be the same, $U\ket{\Psi} = \eta \ket{\Psi}$ for some constant $\eta$ and for every state $\ket{\Psi}$. Furthermore, we know that applying $U$ twice must do nothing, $U^2 = 1$ and so also $\eta^2 = 1$. We now have two cases based on $\eta = \pm 1$. This means the two-particle states do not belong merely to $V\otimes V$ but to one of:
    \begin{align*}
        (V \otimes V)_S &\text{ spanned by } \state \otimes \ket{\phi} + \ket{\phi} \otimes \state \\
        (V \otimes V)_A &\text{ spanned by } \state \otimes \ket{\phi} - \ket{\phi} \otimes \state.
    \end{align*}
\end{example}
Similarly to example~\ref{ex2Particle}, for multiparticle states with $N \geq 2$ we can define $U_(a, b)$ which swaps particles $a$ and $b$ (position and spin). Then on a general $N$-particle state,
\begin{equation*}
    U_{a, b}\ket{\Phi} = \eta_{a, b}\ket{\Phi}
\end{equation*}
where $\eta_{a, b} = \pm 1$. Now for any permutation $\pi$ of $\{1, 2, \cdots, N\}$, we define the operator $U_\pi$ which permutes the particles using $\pi$. On a general state,
\begin{equation*}
    U_\pi \ket{\Psi} = \eta_\pi \ket{\Psi}
\end{equation*}
but the algebra of transpositions implies that $\eta_{a, b} = \pm 1$ with the same value for all pairs $(a, b)$ (see the course IA Groups). Further, since $\pi$ can be obtained as a sequence of transpositions:
\begin{equation*}
    \eta_\pi = 
    \begin{cases}
        1 & \text{ if } \eta_{ab} = 1 \\
        \operatorname{sgn}(\pi) & \text{ if } \eta_{ab} = -1
    \end{cases}
\end{equation*}
